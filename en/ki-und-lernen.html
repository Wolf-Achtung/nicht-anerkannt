The Situation

The best submissions, the worst exams

Three out of four young people now learn with AI. Some achieve top grades with it: they generate mock exams, have their mistakes analyzed, their gaps exposed. Others let the AI write everything — and teachers report the same paradox: the best submissions of all time, the worst exams of all time.

Whether you don't do your homework with AI or without it makes no difference.

The Diagnosis

Whoever hands over the process hands over the skill

The difference is not in the tool. It lies in who keeps the process. Whoever hands over research, structuring, and formulation hands over the skill-building — what remains is a product with no ability behind it.

And there is a second problem: AI is, by design, a confirmation machine. It is trained to appear helpful, to agree, to smooth things over. But learning happens at the point of friction.

You can hand over the product. Not the process.

Our Answer

First you, then the AI

That's why every tool in this Atelier is built the other way round: You write your judgment — only then does the AI examine your thinking. You formulate your thesis — only then come three counter-positions. You bring your text — only then does the AI look for the claim without justification, the missing perspective, the opinion dressed up as judgment. Silent Mode asks nothing but questions. No answers. No confirmation. No finished product to copy.

What everyone googles

The big questions — first you, then our answer

Eight questions everyone is typing into the search bar right now. Take a position — then you get our answer. And a question back.

Is AI making me dumb?

First you:

It's not the AI that makes you dumb — it's the handing over. Whoever outsources research, structuring, and formulation stops training exactly those muscles. Whoever makes the AI contradict, examine, and quiz them instead thinks more sharply afterwards than before. The tool doesn't decide who you become. Your usage does.

Counter: When did your own thinking last surprise you?

Test it in the experiment

Am I unlearning how to think by using AI all the time?

First you:

Possibly — and there's an honest warning signal for it: the recall check. If you can't say what's in your own text without looking at the document, it wasn't your text. Ability grows in the process, not in the product. Test yourself before you reassure yourself.

Counter: Does your last submitted text pass the recall check?

Take the self-test

How do I use AI properly for learning?

First you — what do you let it do?

Four rules: Have it check, not produce. Answer yourself before you see its answer. Demand contradiction instead of agreement. And do the recall check. Everything else is a shortcut — and shortcuts bypass the ability.

To the tools

Can I trust what AI tells me?

First you:

Trust it the way you'd trust a very well-read, very obliging conversation partner: useful, but trained for agreement. It wants to appear helpful — even when contradiction would be more helpful. So don't ask "Is this right?" — ask "What speaks against it?".

Counter: When did you last contradict an AI?

Get some contradiction

Am I at AI's mercy?

First you:

Only if you swap the roles. Whoever asks, leads: as long as you decide what gets examined, doubted, and discarded, the AI works for your thinking — not the other way round. That's exactly what this place is built for.

Counter: In your last AI session, who asked the questions — you or it?

Flip the roles

Can AI still be controlled at all?

First you:

Honest answer: nobody fully knows right now. Current industry studies already count AI in roughly a quarter of all cyberattacks, and there have been first cases of AI systems autonomously — and initially unnoticed — breaking into other machines. Futurologists therefore demand that protection must begin before the invention, not after the loss of control. That's the political level. Your level: control begins with what you yourself examine, doubt, and refuse to adopt unread.

Counter: What control have you already given up voluntarily — for convenience?

Practice judging under uncertainty

Is AI making the world less safe?

First you:

It makes both sides faster: attacks get automated, damages run into the millions per data breach. The best everyday defense is surprisingly old-fashioned — judgment. Whoever is used to examining claims, demanding contradiction, and not reacting instantly to every urgent message is harder to deceive: by humans and by machines alike. Security doesn't start in the firewall. It starts in your head.

Counter: When did you last examine an urgent message instead of reacting to it?

Train the examining eye

Will AI soon replace my thinking — and me?

First you:

It replaces the reproducible: summarizing, formulating, standard knowledge. Judgment, originality, responsibility, and courage become expensive. The question isn't whether AI replaces you — it's whether you're currently training what can't be replaced.

Counter: What can you do that can't be copied?

And School?

The experimental space alongside

This Atelier is not a substitute for school. No degree, no certificate, no accreditation — on purpose. It is the experimental space alongside: a place to find out what learning with and without AI actually does, before it counts in an exam.

Meanwhile, futurologists are publicly demanding exactly this: a radical rebuild of education, a "radical future pedagogy" in which dealing with technological change becomes core curriculum — starting in kindergarten. We're not waiting for the reform. This place is the prototype.

Places like this would have to be built if they didn't exist. We've started.
