For Teachers

Five ready-to-use modules for teaching with the Atelier's thinking tools — AI as sparring partner, not ghostwriter. Free, no login, no data storage.

Why

Tools that push back instead of doing the work

Three out of four young people use AI for homework — and teachers know the paradox: the best submissions of all time, the worst exams of all time. The Atelier's tools are built as the counter-model: the AI only ever responds to the students' own attempt — it examines, asks, and contradicts instead of producing. The full reasoning is here.

No login

No account, no sign-up, no email. Open the link and start — on student devices too.

No data storage

Inputs are not stored server-side. Details in the privacy policy and under AI governance.

Modules

Five modules, ready to go

1. Lesson opener: The Thinking Challenge of the Day — 5–10 min., any subject, age 13+

How it works: Project the question of the day onto the wall. Everyone answers it in exactly one sentence — on paper or in their head. Have two or three answers read aloud, compare briefly: what differs between the answers, even though the question was the same?

Why it works: The questions are deliberately un-googleable and can't be shortcut by AI — there is no correct, only a well-reasoned. Ideal as a thinking warm-up before any subject.

2. Double period: The Contradiction Salon — 90 min., language arts / civics / ethics

How it works: Groups each formulate a thesis on a current topic (15 min.). In the Contradiction Salon, each group generates three AI counter-positions to their thesis (10 min.). Then the core: each group must seriously defend the strongest counter-position — not their own thesis (30 min.). Closing: reformulate the thesis; what changed? (20 min.)

Why it works: The AI supplies the contradiction, but the thinking work — defending, weighing, reformulating — stays entirely with the students.

3. Unit: The Experiment with AI / without AI — 45–90 min., media literacy / language arts

How it works: Everyone individually runs the experiment: the same thinking question, round 1 without help, round 2 with AI sparring, then the version comparison. Afterwards, a class discussion along the three reflection questions: What changed? What was your own thinking, what came from the machine? Who passes the recall check?

Why it works: Instead of talking about AI use, students examine it on their own thinking — the experience replaces the wagging finger.

4. Writing phase: The Text Stress Test — 45 min., language arts / essays / papers

How it works: A first draft of an argumentative text is written without AI (or comes in as homework). Then the Stress Test examines the text for thinking quality: claims without justification, missing counter-arguments, opinion dressed up as judgment. Revision — and only afterwards, if desired, classic style and spelling correction.

Why it works: The rule "first you, then the AI" becomes a writing routine: the machine examines the thinking instead of taking over the writing.

5. Reflection unit: Are You Offloading Your Thinking? — 15–20 min., opener for any AI unit

How it works: Everyone individually takes the self-test (7 questions, anonymous, nothing is transmitted). Then a show of hands: which of the three profiles are in the room? Short discussion: how do you notice, yourself, that you're offloading instead of thinking?

Why it works: The test turns the abstract topic of "AI dependence" into a personal, discussable positioning — without exposure; results stay private.

Good to know

Before you start

The Atelier is not an accredited educational institution and doesn't want to be one — it is the experimental space alongside school (here's why). The AI tools need an internet connection; if the AI is down, the exercises switch to a mode without sparring. Feedback from the classroom is explicitly welcome: contact.
